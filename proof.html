Evidence: the source check on Inspiration4 bloodwork

On a long mission the samples used to check an analyzer expire, so the crew’s own results become the only reference left. This exploratory analysis runs CrewPulse’s source check on public Inspiration4 blood tests: is a change in one person, in what the crew shares, or in the measurement? Every number is recomputed in your browser from NASA’s original files.

1. Red-cell size fell in all four crew the day after landing

Mean corpuscular volume (MCV) measures average red-cell size. At R+1, it fell outside the prototype personal prediction band for all four crew in the same direction, while every value remained inside the 80-100 fL laboratory range. A monitor that only compares people with their own baseline stops here and raises four alarms. CrewPulse asks one more question first.

2. Source check: person, environment or instrument

When the whole crew moves together, the cause is shared. The source check ranks the shared explanations using only what a crew would have on board: the baseline, the related markers, and the timing.

Instrument suspected

    The shift persists

    A three-day flight ended in September 2021. The same crew-wide shift is still present at every later draw:

    DrawCrew averageOutside own normal

    Red-cell size also rose at every preflight draw in all four crew, on the ground. Samples that travel and are run together can move together for reasons unrelated to health.

    What we do not claim. The public files carry no instrument, lot or handling records, so this cannot be proven either way. A lasting change in the crew is not excluded. The fingerprints rank the explanations; on board, a retake or a control sample would settle it. That is the point: without this check, a crew could not tell which question to ask.

    3. A second case at 45 days: potassium up, glucose down

    Blood cells keep using sugar and leak potassium while a sample waits to be processed. At the R+45 draw both markers moved crew-wide, in exactly those opposite directions. Read naively, three of four crew have blood sugar far below the lab range. The source check labels the pair sample handling suspected and holds those eight results, while every other result from the draw stays usable.

    6. Sensitivity to the threshold and the baseline

    SettingR+1 flagsExpected by chanceFalse-flag rateCrew with MCV below own normal

    The app uses the Student t limit, because the typical variation is estimated from only a few draws. At the strict fixed threshold (2.576), about 0.4 flags are expected by chance and 4 appear; three of them are MCV in three different crew members. The fourth is sodium in one crew member, inside the lab range.

    7. False-flag rate

    We tested the method on the preflight draws too, when everyone was healthy and on the ground: each draw left out in turn and compared with the other two. The personal baseline flagged 1.5% of healthy results (6 of 396). The lab ranges flagged 3.8% of the same results. The crew-wide alarm fired on 0 of 102 healthy marker-draws.

    These rates are optimistic: the typical variation is estimated from the same three draws being tested. An independent cohort is step one of the validation plan below.

    How the app treats a single flag

    Even at a 1.5% false-flag rate, 143 results a draw means a couple of false flags every time. CrewPulse shows a single flag as Retake. It asks for a person-level review only when a flag is backed up:

    1. It repeats at the next reading.
    2. It crosses a fixed safety limit too.
    3. It’s crew-wide, which sends it to the source check, not to four separate diagnoses.

    8. Method

    1. Personal normal. The mean of each person’s preflight draws (L-92, L-44, L-3).
    2. Typical variation, s. The within-person preflight standard deviation, pooled across the four crew for each marker.
    3. Drift score. How far a new result sits from the personal normal, in units of typical variation, widened because the normal comes from only a few draws.
    4. Flag. When the drift score is beyond the two-sided 95% Student t limit for the draws behind s: ±2.306 for most markers (8 degrees of freedom).
    5. Crew shift. The average drift score across the crew, scaled by √n. This is the “average of deltas” idea from hospital quality control,[14] with the crew as the patients. It alarms when the average is beyond the same limit and at least half the crew is individually flagged.
    6. Source check. Each crew-wide alarm is scored on five fingerprints: a known handling pair, recurrence at later draws, co-movement in the baseline, whether related markers moved, and timing against the exposure.
    Prototype model class
    d = (x − x̄pre) ÷ ( s · √(1 + 1/n) )    flag if |d| > tdf
    zcrew = mean(d) · √ncrew    alarm if |z| > tdf

    This is a simple Gaussian prediction band centered on a personal baseline. Personalized laboratory intervals are an established research direction,[6] but a clinical reference-change-value calculation also models analytical variation and uses validated analyte-specific biological variation. CrewPulse does not yet do that.

    9. Assumptions

    Stable baseline

    The three preflight draws must represent a healthy steady state. Training load, illness, hydration or circadian timing can move the baseline itself.

    Comparable measurement

    Collection, storage, instrument and laboratory method must be comparable across draws. Preanalytical or batch changes can look like physiology.

    Shared variance is reasonable

    Pooling the within-person standard deviation across four crew stabilizes a tiny sample, but assumes similar day-to-day variability for that marker.

    Residuals are roughly symmetric

    The t limit assumes a simple Gaussian error model. Skewed biomarkers may need transformation or asymmetric limits.

    Flags are not diagnoses

    A drift flag says “different from baseline.” It does not name a condition, its severity, or the correct treatment.

    Fingerprints rank, they don’t prove

    The source check orders the explanations from the evidence on hand. Only a retake, a second device or a control sample can confirm an instrument or handling cause.

    11. Why a source check is needed

    Controls expire

    Analyzers are verified with control samples of known value. The controls NASA flew with its white-cell analyzer last about 3.5 months in cold stowage;[16] NASA lists reagent shelf life among the reasons commercial analyzers fall short for exploration.[17] A Mars mission lasts years.

    It has happened in orbit

    In the ISS validation of that analyzer, the evidence pointed at the control solutions, because a fresh astronaut fingerstick gave the expected values.[18] On Earth in 2026, lots of a handheld blood-analyzer cartridge were recalled for results that read too high.[19]

    Hospitals solved a version of it

    Patient-based quality control uses patients’ own results to catch analyzer shifts.[15] It normally needs thousands of samples. CrewPulse adapts it to a crew of four by leaning on personal baselines. We found no published use of it in spaceflight; that is an absence in our search, not proof.

    Freeze the algorithm, then measure sensitivity, specificity and false-alert burden on unseen repeated-measures data.

    2. Separate sources of variation

    Estimate analytical, preanalytical and within-subject biological variation for each marker and assay.

    3. Link alerts to outcomes

    Define prospective operational endpoints before deriving any Go, limit or No-go threshold.

    4. Build the safety case

    Run human-factors testing, failure-mode analysis, clinician review, cybersecurity assessment and mission-specific verification.

    13. Data check: one inconsistent row excluded

    Red-cell indices are arithmetic from hemoglobin, hematocrit and red-cell count, so they must agree with each other. CrewPulse checks every blood-count row and excludes values from a row that fails, instead of trusting them.

    14. Limits

    Four people, three days

    Long missions may produce larger and different changes, and the whole crew really does shift together in flight. This shows the check can separate the explanations here, not how it performs on Mars.

    Not a clinical RCV

    The model does not separately estimate assay imprecision or use validated analyte-specific biological variation.

    Ground labs and batch effects

    All samples were tested on Earth. A crew-wide shift can reflect handling or instrumentation as well as shared physiology.

    The verdict is a ranking

    “Instrument suspected” means the measurement explanations outscore the others on five simple fingerprints. It is not a finding about any laboratory or device.

    No outcome validation

    No clinical or mission outcome was used to choose the threshold. A flag is a research signal, never a diagnosis or clearance decision.

    Decision rules are simulated

    The Go / No-go logic demonstrates explainability and workflow. It has not been validated for EVA fitness or medical operations.
